# Kill search (claim level) — CL-W44-05: "Model-written adversarial audits do not bound what an optimiser extracts from a simulator: in agent-built simulators that passed three audit rounds, a 600-episode search against the verifier makes at least one in twenty verified successes physically invalid."

**Terms, one gloss each.** *Model-written audit*: a language model reads a simulator's code and reports and searches, by reasoning,
for legal robot actions that break it (the card paper's "audit agent for robustness"). *Budgeted optimiser*: a numerical search
(here CMA-ES, a derivative-free evolutionary optimiser) that runs the simulator a fixed number of episodes and climbs the verifier's
score. *Verifier*: the state-based function that labels an episode a success. *Physically invalid success*: a verifier success in
which a physical invariant (penetration depth, particle mass, energy without actuator work) is violated beyond what the released
honest policy ever produces; the share of such successes is $\epsilon = \#\{\text{success} \wedge \text{violation}\} /
\#\{\text{success}\}$, and the claim's quantity is $\epsilon_{\mathrm{opt}} - \epsilon_{\mathrm{honest}}$. *Audit recall*: the share
of violation classes the optimiser reaches that the audit's own report already names. *Reward hacking*: earning the verifier's reward
without doing what the task intends; *hack-verifiable environment*: an environment with a known hack planted so that hacking is
measured by construction. *Weak-to-strong hardening*: using a weaker model's search budget to repair verifiers against stronger
attackers. *Attack success rate (ASR)*: the share of attempts that earn reward by a hack.

## 0. Verdict in one paragraph

**Killed as written (Gate 2B), narrowed headline survives weakly.** The headline's first clause — a model's pre-release search does
not bound what an optimiser extracts — is the field's stated knowledge in two registers, and the card paper says so itself. In
physics simulation it is the premise of the exploit literature since 2018: Lehman et al. 1803.03453 collect evolution exploiting
simulator bugs; Baker et al. 1909.07528, the very citation the card paper attaches to "any such exploit could otherwise become
learned behavior", write "agents exploiting inaccuracies of the physics simulations to their advantage" and name "methods to generate
environments without these unwanted behaviors" as future work; SPOTA 1907.04685 defines the optimiser's gain from simulator error as
a quantity with an estimator; Kadian 1912.06321 and SAPU 2305.17110 measure single cases; 2605.29032 states that optimisers
"inevitably exploit minor model inaccuracies". In the agent-environment register the exact shape — a model-written audit, a stronger
attacker afterwards, the residual measured — is in print and shipped: Scalable Weak-to-Strong Verifier Hardening 2610.15924
(2026-10-11) runs a hacker–fixer loop with weaker models, then attacks with stronger ones, reports that of 295 tasks Claude Opus 5
could exploit before hardening, 201 (68.1%) resist afterwards (so 31.9% do not), and writes in its own limitations that "Hardening
is bounded by the hacker's search capability and budget. Verifier access supports targeted search but cannot ensure that all
exploit routes are discovered"; an earlier version of the loop "has been adopted by the official Terminal Bench repository as a way
to audit exploits in its environments" — a released audit protocol with an attacker in the loop. ImpossibleRubrics 2609.16816
measures the same quantity for model-generated rubrics against adversarially optimised answers (8–26% exploited, certificate-decided),
and 2606.16062 measures hackability of code RL environments (28.5%). The claim's `grading.gate2` route B surprise ("the field assumes
three audit rounds suffice") is therefore not the field's assumption; nobody in the fetched set claims sufficiency, the card paper
reports a 75% defect rate and warns, and the hardening paper disclaims it. A ruler whose expected reading equals what everyone
already believes is a service (Gate 2B, `taste.md` §3). The claim's `if_true` transfer to RL environment QA ("audits at capability
$c$ certify nothing at $c' > c$") is 2610.15924's research question, answered. What is **not** in print: an optimiser (numerical,
not a language model) run against language-model-written *physics* simulators, with physical invariants rather than a judge
deciding validity, and any simulator pipeline (Gym-Anything, Code2Worlds-2, FATE, DiagGen, WorldSolver) with an optimiser in its
audit loop — their checks are a reading model, a simulator critic on penetration and stability before any optimisation, or a
feasibility solver. That is a mechanism gap (search by execution and score-climbing versus search by reading) on a corpus three days
old, so a narrowed, measurement-register headline survives, weakly, because the domain is new and because the only belief-changing
outcome is the one the probe currently calls its kill: an optimiser that finds nothing. Same verdict from the solo and the lab
position; the lab position turns the null into a sentence in the authors' appendix.

## 1. The question at the concept level

Two questions were asked of the literature. (1) Has anyone shown that an optimiser, RL or search, breaks model-audited,
model-written simulators at a measured rate? Answer: at a measured rate, for model-audited *agent environments and rubrics*, yes
(2610.15924: 31.9% residual; 2609.16816: 8–26%; 2606.16062: 28.5% hackable); for model-written *physics* simulators, no measurement
exists; the physics exploit result is folklore with single-case measurements (Baker, Kadian, SAPU) on hand-built engines. (2) Does any
released audit protocol include an optimiser-in-the-loop check? For agent environments, yes (the Terminal Bench hacker loop; the
hack-verifiable environments of 2605.20744 and 2608.22103 plant known hacks and measure by construction). For simulators, no: the
card paper's robustness auditor "reads the code and searches for legal robot actions"; Code2Worlds-2's simulator critic "inspects
penetration depth, collision pairs, stability, contact forces, joint limits, and robot reachability" inside the generation loop,
before any policy exists; FATE "verifies execution feasibility via simulated embodied interaction" (feasible, not exploitable);
WorldSolver grades agent-written solvers on "Physical Plausibility" as a benchmark. Eureka 2310.12931 is the structural precedent
for an optimiser inside a model's design loop (RL training runs inside each reward-design iteration and its statistics feed back),
but for rewards, not simulators.

## 5. The two closest papers' own text (grep-verified in the fetched text)

- **2610.40565 (card paper; no limitations section exists in the HTML).** Criterion (j), §3: "Robustness to exploits. (j) No sequence
  of legal robot actions causes instabilities, such as non-finite values, or physically impossible states, such as objects flying
  away. Since reinforcement learning optimizes against the simulation, any such exploit could otherwise become learned behavior
  (Baker et al., 2020)." Appendix B: "The audit agent for robustness reads the code and searches for legal robot actions that
  produce non-finite values, energy blow-ups, or physically impossible motion, and returns its evidence to the creation agent."
  §6.4: "The adversarial audit agent (§3) tries to break each simulation using only legal robot actions, and it found a real defect
  in about three quarters of our simulations. ... The creation agent repaired these defects in the next round and verified each
  repair by replaying the audit's own reproduction." Appendix B memory: "after we found colliders that passed audits and appeared in
  renders while having no physical effect, the memory records that a component exists only once a physical probe shows it acting on
  another body." The paper makes no sufficiency claim and reports no residual estimate; its conclusion says RoboWorld "is challenging
  even for frontier models, which fail every test task".
- **2610.15924 (verifier hardening), Appendix F Limitations:** "Hardening is bounded by the hacker's search capability and budget.
  Verifier access supports targeted search but cannot ensure that all exploit routes are discovered. The hardened environments still
  admit successful attacks, and an in-loop robust status is an empirical stopping outcome rather than a security guarantee." And §1:
  "Of the 295 tasks initially exploitable by Claude Opus 5 under unhinted attacks, 201 (68.1%) show zero successful attacks across
  three graded unhinted attempts after hardening." And: "An earlier version of our hacker-fixer loop has been adopted by the official
  Terminal Bench repository as a way to audit exploits in its environments."
- **1909.07528 (third, the premise's source):** "We also found that agents were very skilled at exploiting small inaccuracies in the
  design of the environment, such as seekers surfing on boxes without touching the ground, hiders running away from the environment
  while shielding themselves with boxes, or agents exploiting inaccuracies of the physics simulations to their advantage.
  Investigating methods to generate environments without these unwanted behaviors is another import direction of future research."

Neither closest paper asks for the claim's measurement in so many words. The card paper's open item is not stated; the hardening
paper's open items are its own cohort and compute attribution. The gap (optimiser against model-written physics) is real and is not
one either group has posed.

## 6. Why has this not been done

Enabling pieces, dated: the first released corpus of model-written physics simulators with per-simulation audit reports, shared
audit memory, recorded attempts and released code policies (2610.40565, 2026-10-29, four days old); the hack-verifiable and
