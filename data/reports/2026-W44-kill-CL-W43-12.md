Glossary (each term once). *KV cache*: the per-token key and value vectors an engine keeps in GPU memory so later tokens do
not recompute attention over the prefix. *Residency*: how long, and how much, KV a session holds in GPU memory, including
the intervals when the session is waiting on a tool and generating nothing. *Decode compute*: the arithmetic of producing
output tokens. *Binding resource (bottleneck station)*: the resource whose demand per completed job is largest, so it
saturates first and sets the iteration's throughput; in operational analysis the demand is $D_k = V_k S_k$ (visits times
service time) and throughput is bounded by $X \le 1/\max_k D_k$. *Block-time*: KV blocks held multiplied by the seconds
they are held, the passive-resource demand. *Prefix-cache hit rate*: the fraction of a prompt's tokens whose KV was already
resident, so only the rest is prefilled. *Admission*: the scheduler's decision to let a new session into an engine.
*Goodput*: tokens that reach a gradient per second, as opposed to tokens generated. *Non-additive waste terms*: the lane's
phrase for the operational fact that speeding a non-bottleneck station moves iteration time by less than its share.

## 0. Verdict in one paragraph

**Killed — the headline is the stated premise and the measured result of three RL-systems papers inside the window, and
its lever is shipped scheduler code.** The clause "KV-cache residency, not decode compute" is MISA-T 2608.11152 (August 2026)
Table 1, which lists the dominant cost of agentic rollouts as "Prefix reuse; residency" against "Long decode" for RLVR, and
its section 2.6 writes the demand term the import proposed — a backlog of $N_b$ sessions with mean footprint $\bar k_b$ and
mean residency $T_b$ is "$N_b \bar k_b T_b$ block-time work", where "for agentic sessions, residency includes
tool-execution intervals between turns" (eq. 2, eq. 11) — then measures the consequence on a live rollout service: at a
permissive concurrency ceiling the prefix-cache hit rate collapses from 92.4% to 4.5% and request throughput falls 52.5%
(section 2.5), and residency-aware admission alone cuts mean iteration time 22.8% in a matched 50-iteration training run
while decode throughput rises 38.0% (Table 4), which is what a decode-bound loop cannot do. The per-station demand table
is KV4-RL 2610.14448 (October 2026) Fig. 3(a): weight traffic, GEMM work and KV reads per GPU "normalized using peak HBM
bandwidth and dense BF16 compute throughput so that equal heights represent equal idealized service times", with the
finding that "KV reads constitute the largest idealized service demand throughout the measured traces", that the engine
runs at 91.7 GB of active KV against a 93.9 GB pool, and that once rollout is accelerated the learner "becomes the primary
limit" (Fig. A8) — the non-additivity clause, stated as an observation. The tool-wait term in an agentic RL loop is TideRL
2608.10402 (August 2026) C1: engines evict a paused task's KV while it waits on the environment, the hit rate "plummets and
remains at only ~12%", and task-level admission by KV headroom with pause and resume (CTB) recovers 1.58x hit rate and

(2606.00866) on the serving side. The claim's pre-registered surprise — a 25–35% tool-wait share of KV residency and a

## 4. Why has this not been done

It has been done, in pieces, inside the window, in the neighbours' vocabulary. The enabling pieces landed in order:
asynchronous rollout services at scale (StreamRL April 2025, AReaL May 2025) turned rollout generation into a distributed
serving workload with admission and staleness limits; engines began exposing session-level KV snapshots, prefix-hash events
and Prometheus counters (vLLM Router 2026; SGLang and vLLM endpoints, which TideRL's appendix reads); the field then wrote
the residency term and acted on it within months — Sparse-RL's memory-wall premise (January 2026), TideRL's task-level
admission by KV headroom (August 2026), MISA-T's block-time accounting with tool intervals (August 2026), KV4-RL's
per-station demand chart and KV concentration (October 2026). The exact object the import asked for — a four-station demand
table (decode, KV block-time, sandbox CPU, trainer) on one open agentic stack with the futility clause tested by single-lever
interventions — is unasked because every stack that logs the counters uses them to schedule rather than to publish a table:
MISA-T's controller is the table applied online; the two levers the import would pull are each measured in print (KV kept
through tool waits: TideRL −15.6% step time, MISA-T −22.8% iteration time; the tool wait itself shortened: ObsSpec −9.3% to
−14.2% latency), and the third, trainer speedup under a generation-bound loop, is FlashNext-RL's own 25% trainer-wait number.
Nothing is left for "why now" except FlashNext-RL's specific configuration re-expressed in MISA-T's units, which is a one-day
note.

The pressure evidence routed here (pressure #9 Q04 and Q05, #10 L21/L23/L24) bears on the serving-half accounting and on the
length-censoring claim (CL-W43-08), not on the training-iteration binding station; TurnCost's 98.6% cached share is the
single-tenant regime that MISA-T's admission control reproduces (95–98% hit rates) and TideRL's request-level batching
destroys (12%). The field's assumption in the import's own words — "the loop is bound by generation" — is not what the
neighbours assume: MISA-T, TideRL, KV4-RL and CadenceRL each name KV capacity or KV bandwidth as the generation-side
constraint; "generation" in their usage already means the KV-limited engine.

## 7. Killing-experiment check

Restated. Import S6xF7 pass A: apply the utilization law to FlashNext-RL's published numbers ($0, one day). Pass B: an
8-GPU open stack (prime-rl or verl with a container sandbox), 256 rollouts in flight, five iterations, four demands $D_k$
logged, then two single-lever runs (KV released during tool waits; sandbox latency halved); about 300 GPU-hours. Kill clause:
decode utilization $U_{\mathrm{dec}} \ge 0.85$ with KV below its cap, and KV release moving steady-phase iteration time under
5%. C-W44-08: replay recorded trajectories as $N$ closed-loop sessions with waits $W$ under two KV budgets and four
serializations, about 50 GPU-hours; kill clause: identity arm's hit fraction above 0.55 and per-turn masking's executed
prefill above identity's at $N = 128$, $W = 20$ s, half budget.

Check. Pass B at 300 GPU-hours is 1.8 GPU-weeks, over Gate 1's limit on its own; the claim's Gate 1 was carried by C-W44-08
(50 GPU-hours, under the limit, kill clause well-posed). Neither is run: pass B's positive outcome (KV binds; release moves the
iteration 15–25%) is MISA-T Table 4 and TideRL section 6.3, and its negative outcome is excluded by MISA-T's decode-throughput
rise under admission control; C-W44-08's cliff is MISA-T section 2.5 (hit rate 92.4% to 4.5% at a permissive ceiling) and
TideRL C1 (12% under request-level batching), and its third clause — that published rollout configurations sit above the
cliff — fails on fetched numbers: MISA-T's sweep-tuned baselines and admission policies hold 92–98% hit rates in rollout-only
runs and 74.5–96.2% end-to-end, FlashNext-RL caps KV at 65% with resume at 55%, so the tuned configurations sit below the
cliff by construction and the above-cliff regime is the untuned failure mode the schedulers exist to avoid. What C-W44-08
