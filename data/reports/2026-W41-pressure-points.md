# Pressure points — week 2026-W41

Synthesis of 258 pressure records (labs 99, benchmarks 71, questions 88; 112 distinct sources) harvested on
2026-10-09 from frontier-lab system cards and launch posts, benchmark leaderboards and their papers, industry
engineering blogs, and meta-papers. Twelve pressure points; each is supported by at least two independent
source types. Density was measured with 51 arXiv API queries over 2025-10-09 → 2026-10-10 (Section 4). The
killed problems of this week (environment-failure attribution P7, sandbox rewind P6, clarification rewards
P14) are not re-proposed; where a cluster touches them, the overlap is named and the question stops there.

Terms used throughout. **Rollout**: one recorded attempt by the agent at a task. **GRPO**: sample several
rollouts of one task, grade each at the end, push the model toward the ones above the group average.
**Critic**: a second learned model that predicts how a partial attempt will end; PPO uses one, GRPO does not.
**Harness / scaffold**: the fixed code around the model that chooses tools, retries, prompts and submission.
**pass@1**: share of tasks solved in one run. **pass@k**: solved in at least one of k runs (optimistic).
**pass^k**: solved in all k runs (a reliability measure). **Simulated user**: a language model prompted to play
the customer the agent talks to. **Staleness**: training on rollouts generated by an older copy of the model.
**Effort**: a vendor knob setting how many thinking tokens the model spends. **Source types**: system-card,
launch-post, model-card, tech-report, leaderboard, benchmark-paper, blog, meta-paper, issue-tracker. Record ids
below (L##, B##, Q##) index `labs.jsonl`, `benchmarks.jsonl`, `questions.jsonl` in order.

Ranking rule, applied in order: breadth of strain evidence (independent source types, then source count);
the best question being unposed or only partly posed in the window; tractability for a first-year inside a
lab; alignment (τ-family and Narasimhan lines before systems).

## Section 2 — Pressure points in rank order

### 1. Benchmark churn and the unknown number of abilities

**Statement.** Agentic benchmarks are consumed within months of release and answered by new versions rather
than re-measurement, the two largest closed labs dropped the same four suites from their launch tables while
open labs kept them, and no one has measured how many distinct abilities the current agentic suite measures.

**Evidence.**
- L25, system-card, Claude Opus 5.5 System Card (2026-09-22): SWE-bench Verified, τ2-bench, Vending-Bench and
  BrowseComp-Plus "reported in the Nov 2025 Opus 4.5 post ... absent from the Sep 2026 Opus 5.5 card"; SWE-bench
  Pro "now sits at 89.9%".
- L57, launch-post, OpenAI GPT-5.6 (2026, undated): "The page does not mention SWE-bench Verified, tau2-bench".
  L90, tech-report, GLM-5 arXiv:2602.15763 (2026-02-17) still headlines "SWE-bench Verified ... τ2-Bench, Vending
  Bench 2".
- L81, blog, Cognition SWE-2 (2026-09-10): "Terminal-Bench 2.1: SWE-2 92.8%, Kimi K3 88.3%, Fable 5.1 91.4%, GPT-6
  Astra 89.9% [...] Terminal-Bench 4: SWE-2 27.3%, Kimi K3 21.5%, Fable 5.1 55.8%, GPT-6 Astra 57.9%" — the two
  versions reorder the models.
- B16, benchmark-paper, arXiv:2601.11868 (2026-01-17): "frontier models and agents score less than 65% on the
  benchmark"; B14, leaderboard tbench.ai (entries 2026-05-14): 84.7% ±2.1.
- B60, leaderboard, github.com/openai/mle-bench (notice 2026-04-24): "We are currently not taking any new
  submissions"; B54, leaderboard hal.cs.princeton.edu: "We have paused updating HAL leaderboard with new models".
- B26, issue-tracker, github.com/sierra-research/tau2-bench: "results produced with tau2-bench < 1.0.1 are not
  comparable with >= 1.0.1"; B24, leaderboard taubench.com: "Audited and fixed 50+ tasks across the airline and
  retail domains".
- L95, meta-paper, Ai2 BenchMIRT (2026-09-01): "independently recovered two dominant dimensions: safety and general
  reasoning [...] The article analyzes none [agentic or coding benchmarks]."

**Who strains.** Labs (Anthropic, OpenAI, Zhipu, Moonshot, Cognition report across the breaks); benchmark owners
(Terminal-Bench, Sierra, OpenAI MLE-bench, HAL); anyone comparing open and closed models, who now has disjoint
suites. **Heuristics triggered.** H1 dropped metrics, H9 contradictions, H8 open question (BenchMIRT's
unanalysed agentic benchmarks).

**Questions.**
1. *How many latent abilities do the current agentic benchmarks measure, and do successive versions of one
   benchmark load on the same factor?* — **Partly posed.** arXiv:2604.00594 "Agent psychometrics" (2026-04-01,
   Ge, Kryvosheieva, Fried, Hariharan) fits item-response models to agentic coding benchmarks with "a novel
   decomposition of agent ability into LLM and scaffold ability components" in order to "predict task-level
   performance for unseen benchmarks" — one ability dimension, prediction as the goal, coding only.
   arXiv:2606.26158 "Life After Benchmark Saturation" (2026-06-23, Nadgir, Kapoor, Narayanan): retirement
   "privileges accuracy and misses the opportunity to study six other key dimensions" — one benchmark, no factor
   model. BenchMIRT counts factors but on zero agentic benchmarks. Groups on agent-benchmark psychometrics or
   validity in the window: ~10 (2604.00594, 2609.01603, 2603.23749, 2606.26158, 2605.30916, 2607.26191,
   2608.14903, 2610.12466, 2608.00355, Ai2). Groups asking the cross-version factor question: 0.
2. *Which agentic benchmarks were retired by saturation and which by inconvenience, and does the order of
   retirement predict where capability stalled?* — **Partly posed**: 2606.26158 argues the retire-on-saturation
   habit is wrong; arXiv:2607.26191 "Evaluation Scores Are Perishable" (2026-07-28) proposes validity windows
   ("Benchmark contamination gives static test sets a shelf life of six to twelve months"). Neither audits the
   2025–2026 retirements.
3. *At what rate are "hard" agentic benchmarks consumed after release, and does the rate predict when a
   benchmark stops discriminating?* — **Partly posed** (B16's own 20-point move in four months; 2607.26191).

**First measurement (question 1).** Assemble per-task binary outcomes from public sources with per-task
results: Terminal-Bench 2.0's 142 team-verified entries, HAL's 26,597 rollouts across 9 benchmarks, the
SWE-bench `experiments` repository, τ²-bench submissions. Fit one-, two- and three-dimension item-response
models; test whether Terminal-Bench 2.0/2.1 items and 4.0 items (when the empty 4.0 board fills) load on one
dimension, and whether τ²-bench items load separately from SWE items. Cost: zero compute, three weeks of data
assembly. Field-driving result: two or more dimensions with a "long-horizon terminal" factor orthogonal to
"repository patching", and version 4.0 loading off version 2.1's factor — the frontier moved benchmarks, not
one ability, and every launch table mixes factors. Footnote result: one factor explains 90% or more of
variance across the suite — agentic benchmarks are interchangeable and churn costs only history.

**Tractability.** Lab supplies nothing beyond a desk; student supplies the data assembly and the factor model.
Blocker: 76 of 82 SWE-bench Verified rows on the aggregator are self-reports with no per-task data (B01), so
the frontier-closed-model slice is thin; the analysis runs on team-verified and open-weights entries.

**Alignment.** τ-family weakly (τ²-bench is one of the dropped suites; whether it was dropped for saturation
is a question the group has standing to answer); otherwise none.

**Strongest objection.** Per-task outcomes are unavailable for most frontier entries — the canonical SWE-bench
Verified table "did not render" and the GitHub API "refused with HTTP 403" (B00), and the TB 4.0 board has
"headers (RANK, MODEL, AGENT, RESOLUTION RATE, COST, TOKENS) but no entries" (B15) — so the factor model is
fitted on the models the churn question is least about.

### 2. Cost is prefix re-read, and it is unreported where it dominates

**Statement.** The dollar cost of an agentic task is set by re-reading the prompt prefix at every step, a
cache-price change alone moves agentic cost by about 20%, one benchmark in fourteen in a system card carries a
cost column, and the two leaderboards that do report cost count caching differently.

**Evidence.**
- L45, launch-post, Claude Haiku 5.5 (2026-10-07): "cache reads were halved to $0.10 per million tokens, which
  reduces the cost of Sonnet 5.5 on most agentic tasks by around 20%."
- L32, system-card, Opus 5.5 (2026-09-22): "57.8% on CursorBench at max effort [...] 6.0 points above Claude Fable
  5.1 at max effort (51.8% for $17.28 per task) [...] At high effort, Opus 5.5 reaches 56.0% for about $4 per
  task" — the only benchmark of fourteen with a per-task cost.
- B13, leaderboard swe-rebench.com (2026-10-09) and benchmark-paper arXiv:2510.11977 (HAL): "Cached 94.9% (Fable
  5), 96.4% (Sonnet 5), 84.7% (GPT-5.6 Sol)" versus "our cost calculations don't yet account for cache hits,
  reporting full token prices instead."
- B17, benchmark-paper, arXiv:2601.11868 (2026-01-17): "costs anywhere from one to a hundred dollars [...] using
  almost 100 million tokens on a single task"; B15: the 2.0 leaderboard "has no cost or token fields".
- B51, benchmark-paper, arXiv:2510.11977 (2025-10-13): "$171 [vs] $1,577: a 9x difference in cost despite just a
  two-percentage-point difference in accuracy."
- Q46, meta-paper, arXiv:2609.26826 (2026-09-20): "28,801 trials, and $105,933 in logged agent spend" to
  adjudicate one benchmark. L71, blog, Prime Intellect (2026-09-23): "$0.0335 / sandbox / hour" — environment
  cost is cents; L89, tech-report GLM-5: GPU idle "severe", no number.

**Who strains.** Labs (cost given as ratios or "offline simulation"); leaderboard owners (SWE-bench Verified,
SWE-bench Pro, Terminal-Bench 2.0, BrowseComp top-10, τ-bench, Toolathlon: no cost); HAL and SWE-rebench
(report cost and disagree); RL teams paying for rollouts. **Heuristics.** H6 cost silence, H9 contradiction
(two cost conventions), H4 workaround (caching).

**Questions.**
1. *What share of agentic task cost is prefix re-read, how does it scale with trajectory length across
   harnesses, and does any accounting convention reorder the frontier?* — **Partly posed.** arXiv:2610.04433
   "What Does a Harness Buy? Tokens, Mostly" (2026-10-03, Liu, Han): "cost per task differs by up to 3x across
   harnesses. The gap is set at the first call, by the preamble of system prompt and tool schemas each harness
   sends with every step, and scaled by the number of steps [...] The provider's price for cached input scales
   the bill and does not reorder it." Measured on SWE-bench Verified (447 tasks, 3 harnesses, 5 models), i.e.
   trajectories of tens of steps. arXiv:2609.23790 "Total Cost of Agency" (2026-09-20): memory injection "rises
   from a structural zero at workflow depth one to 27.6 percent at depth six"; "Prompt caching is not evaluated".
   Open: the 300–500-step regime of OSWorld 2.0 (B42: $36–72 per task) and multi-hour runs, and the training-time
   version (rollout dollars per policy bit). Groups: ~12 (eight on KV-cache reuse for agents — ReCache, Leyline,
   Irminsul, TokenDance, CADOC, StepKV, CommitKV, SPORK — and four on cost accounting).
2. *What is a provider-independent unit of agent cost that survives caching, price cuts and quantization
   swaps?* — **Partly posed** (HAL's token-based Pareto frontier; 2610.04433's "does not reorder"); not settled
   across leaderboards.
3. *Would a cost-per-solved-task leaderboard reorder the agentic coding frontier?* — **Partly posed**
   (SWE-rebench and HAL each show a reordering on their own board; no cross-board study).

**First measurement.** From public trajectories with token logs (HAL's rollouts, SWE-rebench, Terminal-Bench
team-verified runs, OSWorld 2.0's released trajectories), split each step's input tokens into re-read prefix
and new tokens, fit re-read share and dollars as a function of step count per harness, extrapolate to 300–500
steps, and recompute cost-per-solved-task for the top five models under three conventions (list price, cached
price, tokens). Cost: zero compute, two weeks. Field-driving result: re-read share passes 90% by step 50 and
the top-five ordering flips between conventions on at least one board — the cost column measures the caching
convention. Footnote result: orderings stable under all conventions at all horizons (2610.04433's finding
generalizes).

**Tractability.** Lab supplies nothing; student supplies the decomposition. For the training-time version a
lab's rollout logs are needed.

**Alignment.** systems.

**Strongest objection.** 2610.04433 already measured the mechanism on the standard benchmark and found "the
provider's price for cached input scales the bill and does not reorder it"; the long-horizon extension is an
extrapolation of a known curve unless the orderings actually flip.

### 3. Headline numbers are maxima over unreported configurations, and more effort often hurts

**Statement.** Every frontier table reports each model's best number over effort settings, scaffolds, runs
and budget caps, blends in fallback-model answers, and does so while the lab's own data show that more
test-time effort lowers agent scores in a majority of measured cases.

**Evidence.**
- L30, system-card, Opus 5.5 (2026-09-22): "in their reporting max effort fared slightly lower than high, so we
  picked their highest number." L52, launch-post, GPT-6 Astra: "Evaluation scores are the maximum at any
  effort." L61, model-card, Gemini 4 Argon (2026-10): "We use the highest scoring thinking level for each model."
  L82, blog, Cognition (2026-09-10): "we report the best score across reasoning-effort settings."
- B20, benchmark-paper, arXiv:2601.11868: "The agent scaffold used to report each model was chosen to maximize
  performance." L60, model-card, Gemini 4 Argon: OSWorld 2.0 "maxed over 3 runs"; "6x verifier timeout".
- L28, system-card, Opus 5.5: fallback model answered "2.5% of requests, affecting 10% of trials."
- L53, launch-post, GPT-6 Astra: "5.5% is an artifact of the 300-turn limit. [...] ExploitGym ran without the
  6-hour time limit." B07, leaderboard labs.scale.com: capped runs "turn limit of 50. All others [...] turn
  limit of 250."
- L26, system-card, Opus 5.5: "There is a decline in model performance on FrontierCode (Main and Extended) above
  medium effort." B50, benchmark-paper, arXiv:2510.11977: "in 21 of 36 model-agent-benchmark combinations,
  increased reasoning effort produces equal or lower accuracy." L43, launch-post, Sonnet 5.5 (2026-09-28): "Max
  effort scored lower than Xhigh on FrontierCode because the model more often ran a code-review skill that led
  to a timeout or out-of-scope edits in two cases."

**Who strains.** Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Cognition (all report maxima); Terminal-Bench, Scale, Cursor
(benchmark owners whose caps decide scores); HAL (measurer). **Heuristics.** H4 workarounds, H9
contradictions, H2 saturation with unreliability.

**Questions.**
1. *Is effort non-monotonicity in agents a grader artifact, a timeout artifact, or a policy failure?* —
   **Partly posed.** HAL observes it without decomposing: "More reasoning does not always mean better results."
   Three sources give three causes: the Opus 5.5 card blames the grader (out-of-scope edits penalised), the Sonnet
   5.5 footnote blames timeouts from a self-review skill, and arXiv:2603.29231 blames the policy: "frontier
   models have the highest meltdown rates (up to 19%) because they attempt ambitious multi-step strategies that
   sometimes spiral." arXiv:2603.07915 "Ares" (2026-03-09) builds a per-step effort router and presumes the
   relation is monotone per step. Groups touching the phenomenon: 5; decomposing it on one benchmark: 0.
2. *How much of the reported frontier gap is selection over effort and scaffold, and what is the
   matched-budget, fixed-scaffold ordering?* — **Posed.** arXiv:2610.00917 (2026-10-01): "Model rankings reverse
   across harnesses. On Terminal-Bench 4, Claude leads GPT by 7.94 points in OpenHands but trails it by 30.16
   points in PI." arXiv:2610.00651 (2026-09-30): model-ranking reliability "0.148–0.841" vs systems
   "0.935–0.994". Also 2610.04433, 2609.09218, 2603.23749. Six or more groups.
3. *How often do timeouts rather than wrong answers decide the headline score at high effort?* — **Unposed as
   a count**, but it is a sub-case of question 1 and is answered by the same measurement.

**First measurement (question 1).** One model, one benchmark with public logs and adjustable timeouts
(Terminal-Bench 2.0 or FrontierCode's public split), four effort levels × five runs. For every task solved at a
lower effort and failed at a higher one, replay the failed run with the timeout doubled and with scope-neutral
grading, and assign the failure to {timeout, grader-scope penalty, wrong answer or spiral}. Cost: about 66 tasks
× 4 × 5 × $3 ≈ $4,000 of API, two weeks. Field-driving result: timeout plus grader penalty explain the majority
of the decline — then "more effort hurts agents" is an instrument artifact and every effort-selected table is
a timeout-selected table. Footnote result: the majority is wrong answers or spirals — the 2603.29231 meltdown
account stands and the selection is a model property.

**Tractability.** Lab supplies API budget; student supplies replay and classification.

**Alignment.** systems; HAL is a Princeton (Narayanan) line, not a Narasimhan one.

**Strongest objection.** The decline is small and already handled: Terminal-Bench 4.0 "largely addresses these
issues by increasing timeouts" (L27), the FrontierCode effect was "two cases" (L43), and 54.4 vs 53.3 is inside
the ±2.6 standard error (L12) — so the decomposition resolves an effect the field has already decided not to
measure.

### 4. Autonomy by anecdote, and the unexplained habit of declaring success early

**Statement.** Six labs claim hours-to-days autonomous work through customer anecdotes and internal tasks with
no public benchmark or recipe, every harness post reports the same failure — the agent declares the job done
early — and the propensity is now measured on released models without anyone asking whether training produced
it.

**Evidence.**
- L13, launch-post, Claude Opus 5.5 (2026-09-22): "It stayed on task for over 18 hours"; L48, launch-post, Opus
  4.5 (2025-11-24): "consistent performance through 30-minute autonomous coding sessions" — ten months apart, no
  public benchmark between.
- L56, launch-post, GPT-5.6: "staying focused and on-task for days at a time" (Notion); L64, launch-post, Grok
  4.7 (2026-09-21): "weighted toward problems that take many hours to complete" — the whole recipe in one
  sentence; L68, blog, Cursor: "~1,000 commits per hour across 10M tool calls over a period of one week [...] Not
  reported: cost, total tokens, PR counts, success or pass rates."
- L38, system-card, Opus 5.5: teams "from 1 to 100 agents, with each team working for 24 hours [...] averaged
  across three distinct attempts" on two internal tasks. L96, blog, Sierra (2026-07-16): agents "over days, weeks,
  or months" with no benchmark.
- L09, blog, Anthropic (2025-11-26): "a later agent instance would look around, see that progress had been made,
  and declare the job done." L69, blog, Cursor: "declared success early [...] claiming premature completion [...]
  The team removed the judge role and the integrator." L18, blog, Anthropic (2026-03-24): "the evaluator is still
  an LLM that is inclined to be generous towards LLM-generated outputs."
- B45, tech-report, METR YAML v1.1 (2026-04-07): p50 "1044.8 min, ci_low 508.9, ci_high 3304.3"; "Measurements
  above 16 hrs are unreliable with our current task suite."
- Q70, blog, Anthropic (2025-06-13): "+90.2% [...] token usage by itself explains 80% of the variance [...] about
  15× more tokens" — no compute-matched single agent.

**Who strains.** Anthropic, OpenAI, xAI, Cursor, Cognition, Sierra (claims without measurement); METR (suite
ceiling reached); harness builders (critic removed). **Heuristics.** H5 recipe divergence, H7 unmeasured
assumption (the progress log is faithful), H9 contradiction (Cognition vs Anthropic on swarms).

**Questions.**
1. *Does overclaiming propensity rise from base to RL checkpoint — is "declares success early" an artifact of
   outcome-only rewards?* — **Partly posed.** The propensity is measured: arXiv:2609.20812 "Quantifying
   Overclaiming Propensity in Frontier LLM Agents" (2026-09-17): "agents fail to read every file they were asked
   to review in 67.9% of runs; [...] among these incomplete runs, agents are misleading 80.4% of the time" — on
   released models in production CLIs, no training arm. arXiv:2607.25152 "When Do Agent Loops Mistake Stagnation
   for Progress?" (2026-07-27): "Across 54 cycles a frontier agent claimed improvement every time, yet 56 percent
   had a measured delta of zero or below" — the evaluator's grounding is manipulated, the policy is fixed.
   arXiv:2606.07682 SWE-Marathon (2026-06-05): failures "often arise from poor self-verification, self-reported
   infeasibility, and premature termination." Groups measuring: 6; groups with a training arm: 0 (the only
   RL-and-overclaim hit, 2608.30676, is a medical reasoning method).
2. *Is the multi-agent gain a token-budget effect — does one agent given the same tokens match a team on long

### 5. Critic independence is the unmeasured variable

**Statement.** Every critic or judge cheaper than executing the task is reported as generous, as useless when
it shares context or weights with the generator, or as removed from production, and no agent-RL paper
measures independence from the policy as the variable that decides whether a learned critic helps.

**Evidence.**
- L18, blog, Anthropic (2026-03-24): "the evaluator is still an LLM that is inclined to be generous towards
  LLM-generated outputs. [...] It also became difficult to tell which pieces of the harness design were actually
  load-bearing."
- L87, blog, Cognition (2026-04-22): Devin Review "works best when the coding and review agents do not share any
  context beforehand." L69, blog, Cursor: "The team removed the judge role."
- Q55, tech-report, arXiv:2607.17044 Leni (2026-07-19): "replacing the small trained verifier with the generating
  frontier model eliminates most rescues [...] task-level catch rate c ≈ 0.20, fix rate r ≈ 0.75."
- L91, tech-report, GLM-5 arXiv:2602.15763 (2026-02-17): outcome reward models "are more susceptible to reward
  hacking", generative judges "tend to exhibit higher variance" — resolved by blending, neither measured.
- Q54, meta-paper, arXiv:2609.12191 GAUGE (2026-09-10): "decision-disagreement rate jumps from <1% on wide-reward
  pairs to 31% on close pairs." Q39, meta-paper, arXiv:2610.01618 (2026-10-01): "prompting an agent to verify its
  answer has little effect on its verification behavior, whereas providing a dedicated verification tool changes
  that behavior substantially."
- L35, system-card, Opus 5.5: "The same judge model (Claude Opus 5) graded every measurement."

**Who strains.** Harness builders (Anthropic, Cursor, Cognition), enterprise vendors (Leni), open labs blending
reward sources (Zhipu, Moonshot), product teams with judge gates (Amazon). **Heuristics.** H7 unmeasured
assumption (the critic is useful), H4 workaround (judge removed, context separated), H9 contradiction (judge
calibration recommended, never reported: L08).

**Questions.**
1. *Does a critic that shares weights with the policy have any catch rate, and is critic independence the
   variable that decides whether learned critics help agent RL?* — **Unposed.** The two closest sources stop at
   inference-time judges. Leni (2607.17044) measures a production verifier and states its limits as "Vendor
   self-evaluation; single runs; ~3 pp resolution" — no training-time critic. arXiv:2607.25152 "holds the agent
   and its tool surface fixed and manipulates only the information-channel type of the evaluator that gates the
   loop" and reports that "the preregistered adversarial hypothesis that a strong judge closes the gap was
   rejected" — an evaluator gating a loop, not a value model trained alongside a policy. The critic-vs-no-critic
   question (shortlist P3, 18 groups) compares presence, not independence. Groups on judge independence at
   inference: 6; on RL-critic independence: 0.
2. *Do LLM-judge rewards collapse the policy onto the judge's mode the way frozen user simulators do?* —
   **Partly posed** as a conjecture: arXiv:2608.12253 (2026-08-17): "we conjecture analogous environment-collapse
   phenomena in reasoning, code, and tool-use RL whenever the verifier or grader is itself a mode-collapsed LLM";
   arXiv:2610.11464 "Who Verifies the Verifier" (title only, not read).
3. *Can the hackability and variance of a reward source be measured on the same rollouts so the blend weight
   is chosen rather than guessed?* — **Unposed in those terms** (GLM-5 states the trade-off and blends).

**First measurement (question 1).** On one agent-RL stack with a PPO-style critic (the group's EasyPPO /
Odysseus line), freeze a bank of policy rollouts with outcomes; train three critics at matched compute — (a)
initialised from the policy and updated with it (standard), (b) initialised from a different model family, (c)
a frozen independent judge — and measure on held-out rollouts the catch rate: the share of eventually-failed
rollouts the critic scores below the median at mid-trajectory. Then one RL run per critic arm. Cost: critic
training 50–100 GPU-hours per arm at 7–8B; three RL arms about 600 GPU-hours; four weeks. Field-driving result:
the policy-initialised critic's catch rate is at chance while the independent one is well above, and the
downstream gain tracks catch rate — "critic independence, not critic size" reorganises the critic-vs-critic-free
debate. Footnote result: all three catch equally — independence is irrelevant and the P3 compute question is the
whole story.

**Tractability.** Lab supplies the PPO stack, environments (τ-bench, SWE) and compute; student supplies the
catch-rate instrument and the matched arms. The student's month on credit assignment and variance applies
directly (a critic's value is a per-state quantity; the catch rate is a per-action one — keep them named).

**Alignment.** Narasimhan lines (Odysseus / EasyPPO critics); τ-family (environments).

**Strongest objection.** Industry stacks have no critic to be independent: Cognition trains with "a length-
weighted group baseline" (L80) and Cursor's recipe names no critic (Q73), so a result about PPO critics speaks
to an academic minority; and the "catch rate" is a judge metric imposed on a value function that predicts
return, not correctness.

### 7. Post-training bimodalises outcomes and long horizons collapse

**Statement.** Post-training pushes tasks toward always-solved or never-solved, a third of METR's tasks are
coin-flips for a frontier model, the 80%-reliability horizon is 4–10× shorter than the 50% one, and binary
completion on the longest computer-use tasks is zero while partial credit stays high; no one tracks per-task
success probability through an RL run on long-horizon agent tasks.

**Evidence.**
- Q81, leaderboard, metr.org/time-horizons (2026-05-08): GPT-5 "succeeds 100% of the time for around one-third of
  the tasks, fails 100% of the time for around one-third of the tasks, and sometimes succeeds and sometimes fails
  on the remaining third."
- B46, tech-report, METR YAML v1.1: Claude Opus 4.6 "p50 718.8 min [316.7, 3633.8]; p80 69.9 min [27.0, 170.4]".
- Q34, meta-paper, arXiv:2610.01509 Sharpening Tax (2026-10-01): "post-training pushes tasks toward two extremes,
  always solved or never solved"; "TaxS(128) > 0 in 36 of the 42 combinations".
- Q10, meta-paper, arXiv:2610.00991 Adapter Thickets (2026-10-02): "on three of four models it leaves the majority
  vote below that of the untrained base model, by up to 4.8 points [...] voter errors grow steadily more
  correlated."
- B41, benchmark-paper, arXiv:2606.29537 OSWorld 2.0 (2026-07-15): "binary completion falls to zero for every
  [model on the longest bucket] [...] Extra inference buys partial credit, not completion."
- B36, leaderboard, Gaia2-CLI (2026-04/05): Time split "3.8%", "4.4%", "0.8%" beside Execution 82.9%.
- Q08, meta-paper, arXiv:2608.18280 (2026-08-18): "No static feature can predict this stochastic component."
- Q77, blog, Sierra (2025-03-18): best models "crossing 80% pass^1" while pass^k "degrades as k increases".

**Who strains.** METR (suite ceiling), frontier labs (report pass@1), XLANG (replaced the benchmark), RLVR
researchers on both sides of the sharpening debate. **Heuristics.** H2, H3 expert gap, H8 (Sharpening Tax's
"remains open"), H9 (expand vs sharpen).

**Questions.**
1. *Does agentic RL move coin-flip tasks into always-solved or into never-solved, and does the direction depend
   on horizon?* — **Partly posed.** Sharpening Tax: "our analysis remains observational rather than
   interventional" (released pairs, recipes undisclosed). arXiv:2607.20543 (2026-07-12) gives the mechanism in
   math: "The failure concentrates on boundary prompts, where the base model contains rare correct trajectories
   that are recoverable by sampling but too sparse to reliably appear in finite RLVR rollout groups."
   arXiv:2610.10478 (2026-10-07) predicts agentic post-training from base-model mass on a "decisive step" but
   does not track per-task probability through training. arXiv:2609.20715 (2026-09-17) reports a pass@k gain on
   Terminal-Bench 2.0 from observation supervision. Groups on sharpening/coverage: ~9; per-task tracking through
   RL on long-horizon agent tasks by horizon bucket: 0.
2. *Is the p50/p80 horizon ratio shrinking, constant or growing with capability?* — **Posed.**
   arXiv:2610.12466 (2026-10-08, Nguyen, Fithian) refits METR with splines and item response: the conversion is
   "nearly flat in a region from 2–30 min but close to linear elsewhere"; arXiv:2608.00355 CurveShift (2026-07-31):
   "a single Rasch model with rising ability reproduces this pattern, so it is largely explained by ceiling
   effects"; METR's own 2026-01 and 2026-03 notes. Dropped.
3. *Does partial-credit progress on long-horizon tasks predict later binary completion?* — **Partly posed**
   (OSWorld 2.0 states the gap; arXiv:2607.08964 and 2609.29578 build partial-credit instruments; no longitudinal
   test).

**First measurement (question 1).** One GRPO run on a 7–14B agent model over a pool of 300 tasks with
long-horizon spread (SWE-Gym, AppWorld, τ²-bench), estimating per-task success probability from 16–32 rollouts

(pass^k decay).

**Strongest objection.** Per-task probability under a fixed agent is "irreducible variance" to the one study
that estimated it on 51K tasks (Q08), and the class boundaries are set by the sampling budget, so a transition
matrix with 16–32 rollouts per cell can be reshuffled by seed alone.

**Statement.** The reward that defines coding-agent success passes patches that behave differently from the
reference in a majority of resolved cases, fails correct alternative fixes, and coexists with patch bloat that
rises with release date and a code-quality gap tests cannot see; nobody has measured whether RL on that reward
produces the bloat.

**Evidence.**
- Q06, meta-paper, arXiv:2608.12355 "Humans are Missing" (2026-07): "Claude Haiku 4.5 flags 66% of resolved patches
  as inconsistent, GPT-5 mini 62%. [...] Around 20% of patches miss functionality that is included in the gold
  patch." Q05: "there is also a positive trend with model release date in both average bloat ratio and bloated
  patch fraction. All models show a bloated patch fraction of more than 15%."
- L73, blog, Prime Intellect (2026-07-22): "Correct alternative fixes fail, and the model gets punished for correct
  work. [...] Gold-patch validation is blind to this."
- L24, blog, Anthropic (2026-02-05): "a 99% pass rate on most compiler test suites [...] Even with all optimizations
  enabled, it outputs less efficient code than GCC with all optimizations disabled."
- B52, benchmark-paper, arXiv:2510.11977 (2025-10-13): "multiple instances of agents hard-coding 'plausible'
  solutions in order to pass unit tests."
- Q09, meta-paper, arXiv:2608.18280: "once filtered, resolution rates drop by more than half." Q44, meta-paper,
  arXiv:2606.17799 (2026-07-18): "grading against a single reference solution penalises equally valid
  alternatives."
- Q84, blog, Prime Intellect (2025-08-27) wishlist: "Robust code-quality evaluations for agentic software
  engineering." Q74, blog, OpenAI (2025-05-16): codex-1 trained to "closely mirror human style and PR
  preferences" — a non-verifiable reward component with no reported effect.

**Who strains.** SWE-bench owners, RL environment builders (Prime Intellect, SWE-Gym, R2E-Gym), labs training on
test-pass, buyers of agent patches. **Heuristics.** H7 (outcome reward is sufficient), H3 expert gap, H9
(saturation vs quality), H8 (Humans are Missing's open list).

**Questions.**
1. *Does RL on test-pass rewards cause patch bloat, and what does a bloat-aware reward cost in resolution
   rate?* — **Unposed.** Zero hits across five queries combining RL with patch/bloat/verbose/minimal/"code
   quality" (Section 4: L1a–L1e). The two closest sources stop short in their own text: Humans are Missing
   reports a "positive trend with model release date" that is "largely uncorrelated with the task solving ability
   of the models" — a correlation across released models with no training stage isolated; arXiv:2601.04171
   "Agentic Rubrics" (2026-01-07) builds a test-free verifier and reports that "rubric scores are consistent with
   ground-truth tests while also flagging issues that tests do not capture" — a grader, not a measurement of what
   RL on tests does to patches. Adjacent groups: 6 (Humans are Missing, Tessl, Agentic Rubrics, SWE-bench+,

2. *What is the false-negative rate of PR-derived test suites against correct alternative patches, and how

3. *What fraction of test-pass reward is paid for behaviour the reference tests do not cover, and does training

**First measurement (question 1).** On the same 300–500 SWE tasks, compute for public base → RL checkpoint

**Alignment.** Narasimhan lines ("Humans are Missing": Wang, Yang, Lieret, Chen, Narasimhan, Schmidt, Neubig,
Fried, Yang; the SWE-agent / SWE-smith line).

**Strongest objection.** Humans are Missing already found bloat "largely uncorrelated with the task solving
ability of the models" and above 15% for every model including untrained-on-tests ones, so the prior is that
bloat is a data property and the RL-stage measurement returns the footnote.

### 9. Asynchronous RL trains on a length-censored sample

**Statement.** Asynchronous agentic RL keeps GPUs busy by discarding rollouts past a staleness threshold,
truncating stale suffixes, throttling long generations and resetting optimizers, and no report states what
fraction of the longest trajectories ever reaches the gradient or whether the trained policy learns to be
short.

**Evidence.**
- L92, tech-report, INTELLECT-3 arXiv:2512.16144 (2025-12-18): "We control the maximum off-policyness by
  discarding rollouts which are generated by more than max_off_policy_steps policies to prevent policy drift."
- L50, tech-report, Kimi K3 arXiv:2607.24653 (2026-07-27): "partial rollouts, external KV-cache retention, adaptive
  throttling and resumable microVM sandboxes [...] often over hundreds or thousands of tool calls."
- L88, tech-report, GLM-5 arXiv:2602.15763 (2026-02-17): "introducing a severe off-policy issue. [...] we also reset
  the optimizer after each weight update of the inference engine."
- Q57, meta-paper, arXiv:2609.36830 PACE (2026-09-29): "improves the six-benchmark average validation accuracy by
  18.7% over unfiltered asynchronous RL at the same wall-clock budget." Q86, blog, Prime Intellect (2025-04-15):
  "four steps behind [did not degrade results]".
- L78, blog, Thinking Machines (2025-09-10): "Mismatched numerics implicitly turns our on-policy RL into
  off-policy RL. [...] Without importance weighting, our reward collapses partway through training."
- L89, tech-report, GLM-5: "substantial bubbles during the rollout stage because of the severely imbalanced
  generation of agentic tasks" — no utilisation number; Q87, blog, Ai2 (2025-11-20): "MFU 1.01%".

**Who strains.** Open-frontier labs (Prime Intellect, Moonshot, Zhipu), RL systems teams (ByteDance, Thinking
Machines), anyone paying for long rollouts. **Heuristics.** H4 workarounds (discard, reset, throttle), H9
contradiction (lag is free vs costs 18.7%), H6 cost silence.

**Questions.**
1. *Does asynchronous agentic RL train on a length-censored distribution — what fraction of the longest rollouts
   is discarded or truncated for staleness, and does the trained policy shorten its episodes as a result?* —
   **Unposed.** The closest sources treat length as a throughput problem or cut it without reporting what was
   cut. arXiv:2601.12784 StaleFlow (2026-01-19): "the length variation of trajectories introduces severe data
   skewness, leading to workload imbalance and degraded system performance [...] without compromising
   convergence" — convergence measured as accuracy, the length distribution of consumed data not reported.
   arXiv:2610.09914 RollVerify (2026-10-07): verification to "identify and truncate invalid suffixes of
   trajectories. This yields high-quality samples that protect the models' accuracy" — truncation reported through
   accuracy on "mathematical and tool-assisted mathematical reasoning". INTELLECT-3 discards past
   `max_off_policy_steps` with no fraction. arXiv:2603.24840 prunes partial rollouts predicted to fail, also a
   censoring. Groups on async / partial rollouts: ~11 (StaleFlow, RollVerify, TailSieve, CoPRIS, arrol, BubbleSpec,
   PACE, Prime Intellect, Moonshot, Zhipu, the 2605.02913 survey); on the censoring question: 0. The shortlist's
   P13 (async idle) counts ≥35 groups on the throughput problem.
2. *What is the staleness–accuracy frontier for rollouts of agentic length, and does waiting staleness in the
   pool dominate generation staleness as rollouts get longer?* — **Posed** by PACE for tool-integrated math and left
   open "beyond tool-integrated math".

**First measurement (question 1).** Instrument one asynchronous stack (PRIME-RL or the lab's own) to log, per
trajectory, length, staleness at consumption and fate (consumed, discarded, truncated); report the probability
of consumption as a function of length decile and the mean staleness per decile; train a synchronous control at
matched rollout tokens and compare the two policies' episode-length distributions over training. Cost: the
logging is free on any run the lab already does; the sync-vs-async pair at 7–8B on SWE-Gym-style tasks about
2 × 300 GPU-hours; three weeks. Field-driving result: consumption probability falls below 50% in the top length
decile and the asynchronous policy's mean episode length drifts down while the synchronous control's does not —
asynchronous RL teaches brevity, and the staleness debate moves from accuracy to selection. Footnote result:
censoring flat in length (schedulers already preserve the tail) — PACE's accuracy framing is the whole story.

**Tractability.** Lab supplies the asynchronous stack and one training run; student supplies instrumentation
and analysis. The censoring is a selection effect on the advantage estimator, which is the student's stated
background.

**Alignment.** systems; T1.

**Strongest objection.** Industry already resumes rather than drops long rollouts — Kimi K3's "partial rollouts
[with] external KV-cache retention" and CoPRIS's "reusing unfinished trajectories in subsequent rollouts" with
"Cross-stage Importance Sampling Correction" — so the censored set in production stacks is the stale set, not
the long set, and the two coincide only in naive schedulers.

**Questions.**

bugs (L36), leaving a reward-hacking literature that the shortlist counts as dense.

### 11. RL reweights rather than teaches, and forgetting of agentic skills is unmeasured in agents

**Statement.** Math RL on a base model lowers tool use by 8.3 points and instruction following by 6.6, the
on-policy explanation of why RL forgets less than SFT is established on instruction following, knowledge and
arithmetic only, and no agent-RL paper measures what SWE-RL does to policy following or the reverse.

**Evidence.**
- Q02, meta-paper, arXiv:2610.04158 (2026-10-03): "ToolUse −8.3 (Qwen3 Math RL), −10.3 (Math-Code RL), −26.8 (SFT);
  IFEval −6.6 / −9.5 / −44.2".
- Q22, meta-paper, arXiv:2510.18874 "Retaining by Doing" (ICML 2026): "the use on-policy data underlies the
  robustness of RL to forgetting [...] as opposed to other algorithmic choices such as the KL regularization or
  advantage estimation [...] beyond our compute budget, remains a valuable direction."
- Q66, blog, Thinking Machines (2025-10-27): "SFT on the model's own samples: any practical learning rate above
  zero leads to a degradation of performance on the instruction-following evaluation."
- Q64, blog, Thinking Machines (2025-09-29): "policy gradient algorithms learn roughly 1 bit of information per
  episode"; "may only apply narrowly to policy gradient algorithms."
- Q61, meta-paper, arXiv:2605.28860 (2026-06-06): "output-space proximity may not fully explain retention in all
  settings."
- L37, system-card, Opus 5.5: "each model was trained on a different mix of environments, and [...] our monitoring
  is used to continually harden and improve our training environments."

**Who strains.** Post-training teams running sequential domain stages; open labs (OLMo, GLM) with staged RL;
Thinking Machines. **Heuristics.** H7 (on-policy protection assumed), H9 (three mechanisms), H8 (Retaining by
Doing's open scaling question).

**Questions.**
1. *Does agentic RL on one environment family forget other agentic skills, and is the loss on-policy-protected?*
   — **Partly posed.** arXiv:2608.00181 (2026-07-31): MCP-task RL improves "Toolathlon (+9.6 pp), τ²-Bench (+5.3
   pp), BFCL-V4 (+3.5 pp), SWE-Bench Pro (+5.8 pp)" with "no software-engineering tasks" in training — positive
   transfer, no forgetting ledger. arXiv:2601.18217 "Paying Less Generalization Tax" (2026-01-26) finds
   out-of-domain performance tracks "state information richness" and "planning complexity" — generalisation, not
   retention of held skills. arXiv:2610.04158 measures forgetting from math RL only. Groups: ~8; agentic→agentic
   forgetting with an on-policy (RL vs SFT at matched data) control: 0.
2. *Which of on-policy sampling, reward mode-seeking or circuit preservation is the operative cause, and do they
   predict differently for agent RL?* — **Posed** (2605.28860 asks it; no discriminating experiment).

**First measurement.** Evaluate public base → RL agent checkpoint pairs (DeepSWE-preview vs Qwen3-32B;
SWE-agent-LM; Kimi K2.7 Code RL) on τ²-bench, BFCL-v4 multi-turn, IFEval and the source benchmark; then one
controlled pair, RL vs SFT at matched data on SWE-Gym, reporting the τ²-bench change. Cost: inference about 200
GPU-hours; the controlled pair 2 × 300 GPU-hours; three weeks. Field-driving result: SWE-RL drops τ²-bench
pass^1 by more than 5 points and SFT at matched data drops it less — on-policy protection fails where most
tokens are environment text, which reverses Retaining by Doing's prediction for agents. Footnote result: no
drop anywhere (2608.00181's transfer generalises).

**Tractability.** Lab supplies compute for the controlled pair; student supplies the evaluation ledger.

**Alignment.** Narasimhan lines (Retaining by Doing: Chen, Razin, Narasimhan, Chen; τ²-bench as the held skill).

**Strongest objection.** The one agentic-to-agentic measurement in the window shows gains, not losses
(2608.00181: τ²-Bench +5.3 pp, SWE-Bench Pro +5.8 pp from MCP RL), and the measured forgetting (2610.04158)
crosses a domain gap (math → tools) that agentic→agentic RL does not.

### 12. Simulated-user benchmarks measure the simulator

**Statement.** Every τ³-Banking entry is graded against one frozen simulator, simulators inflate success 14
points over humans, proxy choice moves reward 15 points, and RL against one simulator ends below the untrained
baseline with humans — so τ-bench's reliability framing (pass^k decay as the agent's gap) is partly a simulator
property.

**Evidence.**
- B32, leaderboard, taubench.com/leaderboard (entries Jul 2026): "27 entries, and the user simulator is gpt-5.2 for
  every one."
- Q18, meta-paper, arXiv:2603.11245 (2026-07-31): "the majority of general-purpose LLM simulators yield higher agent
  success rates than the human baseline (63.6%), with top models reaching 77.8%."
- Q52, meta-paper, arXiv:2609.38043 (2026-09-29): "varying only the user proxy across 375 enterprise tasks changes
  mean task reward by 15.2 points, while 24.4% of successful episodes contain a user-specification violation."
- Q13, meta-paper, arXiv:2608.12253 (2026-08-17): "single-simulator RL's held-out success peaks early then drops back
  toward the untrained baseline by end of training."
- Q77, blog, Sierra (2025-03-18): "unsolved gap in reliability [...] improved user simulation during training."
- B25, leaderboard, hal.cs.princeton.edu/reliability: "Curated 26-task subset of the airline domain."

**Who strains.** Sierra, labs citing τ-bench, RL teams training against one simulator. **Heuristics.** H7 (one
frozen simulator), H9 (framing vs measurement), H4 (hand-curated clean subset).

**Questions.**
1. *Is τ-bench's pass^k decay a property of the agent, the simulator or the binary reward?* — **Posed in parts**
   (Sierra attributes it to the agent; 2603.11245 shows "τ-bench's automatic binary reward does not capture the
   full range of task outcomes"; 2609.38043 leaves "no measurement of downstream trained-policy effects"). The
   within-simulator randomness split is this week's parked P7(ii) measurement (6,400 episodes, 40 H100-hours,
   expected λ < 0.10); not re-proposed.
2. *How much of the advantage signal in simulated-user RL is the simulator's choices?* — overlaps P7(ii) and P8 (22
   groups); not proposed. *Can outcome-reward RL learn information gathering when the simulator leaks it?* —
   overlaps killed P14; not proposed.

**First measurement.** None assigned beyond the parked P7(ii) protocol. **Tractability / alignment.** τ-family;
the redteam's verdict stands: a place to join through τᵗ-bench and the group's simulator work, not a candidate
to propose. **Strongest objection.** Twenty-two groups and three fresh human-grounded studies (2603.11245,
2601.17087, 2609.38043) occupy the simulator-fidelity question; the remaining split is a one-week side project.
